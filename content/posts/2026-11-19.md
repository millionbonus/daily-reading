## 1. Taxi Drivers Say 70 Is Too Young to Stop

More than 100 taxis drove in a convoy to the Ministry of Labor in Taipei this week. The drivers were protesting a rule that stops professional drivers from working once they turn 70. Many of them say they are still healthy and want to keep earning a living.

Chang Chi-yu, a spokesman for the Taiwan All-People Taxi Drivers Association, called the rule discriminatory. He said it breaks drivers' constitutional rights to work and to be treated equally. He also pointed out a gap in the law. The Labor Standards Act lets employers and workers agree on a later retirement age. However, road traffic rules still require professional drivers to stop at 70.

The drivers also raised a second issue: their job status. Taxi companies give them employment certificates, so Chang argued that they should count as employees. At the moment, many are treated as independent contractors. That means they do not get employer-paid pensions, insurance contributions, paid leave or severance pay.

The ministry said it would pass the age-limit demand to the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, which controls the rule. That ministry raised the maximum age from 68 to 70 in 2017. On job status, the Labor Ministry said the answer depends on each contract and on how much control the company has.

## 2. Stan Shih Returns to APEC to Talk About AI

Stan Shih, the founder of the computer company Acer, left Taiwan on Wednesday for the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting in Shenzhen, China. APEC is a group of Asia-Pacific economies that works on trade and cooperation. The meeting runs for two days.

Taiwan's president does not attend these meetings, because Beijing objects. Instead, he sends a representative. This year, President Lai Ching-te asked Shih to go. It is Shih's first time as Taiwan's representative in almost twenty years. He last attended in Sydney in 2007, where he suggested a plan to narrow the digital divide, the gap between people who have good technology and people who do not.

At the airport, Shih said he would share Taiwan's experience with AI and digital change. He described Taiwan as "an indispensable partner" in global technology and AI supply chains. A supply chain is the network of companies that makes and delivers a product. He also said that fast digital change is reshaping industries across the region. In his view, Taiwan's government and businesses could invest overseas to help the AI industry grow and share its benefits.

President Lai asked Shih to say that Taiwan is ready to work with partners on AI and on a supply chain that is strong and reliable.

## 3. Tiny Trackers Help Scientists Follow Butterflies

Scientists in eastern Taiwan have tagged two butterflies with tiny transmitters, a first in Asia according to the officials involved. Each transmitter weighs just 0.06 grams. The East Longitudinal Valley National Scenic Area Headquarters announced the project on Wednesday, together with conservation groups.

The two species are the small crow and the dark blue tiger. In the past, researchers studied butterfly migration by putting tags on the insects and hoping to catch them again later. Deputy director Hung Chao-chang said this method gave only fragmented records, with many gaps. The new tags work differently. They use solar power and send a wireless signal that a mobile phone can pick up through Bluetooth. This lets researchers detect the same butterfly many times and follow its journey from the day it emerges until it dies.

Ordinary people can help, too. They can install an app called "Project Monarch" and turn on Bluetooth and location services. When a tagged butterfly flies close by, the app records it. Users can then send in a photo, the butterfly's ID number, the time and the place. This kind of volunteer work is called citizen science. Chan Chia-lung of the Taiwan Insect Habitat Protection Association said a similar US project fitted 400 monarch butterflies with trackers two years ago. Those butterflies were followed on their long trip from North America to Mexico.
