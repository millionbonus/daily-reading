## 1. Taiwan Promises Stronger Defense After G7 Message

Taiwan's government said on Thursday that it will keep building up its self-defense. It also promised to strengthen what it calls "whole-of-society resilience." This means that ordinary people, businesses, and public services all prepare to cope with a crisis.

The announcement followed a joint statement from the foreign ministers of the G7 group of countries. They met in Paris on November 9 and 10, together with the European Union's top foreign-policy official. In the statement, they stressed the importance of peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait. They also said they were concerned about China's increasingly assertive activities in the waters east of Taiwan. They opposed any unilateral change to the status quo, especially by force or by threat of force. And they encouraged both sides to settle their differences peacefully, through dialogue.

Presidential Office spokesperson Karen Kuo said peace in the Taiwan Strait and the wider Indo-Pacific region is vital to global security and prosperity. She thanked G7 members for watching cross-strait developments closely. She also thanked them for supporting Taiwan's meaningful participation in international organizations. A spokesperson for the Executive Yuan, Michelle Lee, said the government welcomed the statement.

## 2. The FAA Brings Its AI Flight-Routing Tool to the Whole Country

The United States aviation authority, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), is expanding an artificial intelligence tool to the entire country. The tool is called SMART, short for Strategic Management of Airspace Routing Trajectories. It cost about $875 million.

SMART does not fly planes. It looks at traffic in the sky and suggests better routes. The FAA first used it in the airspace around Washington, D.C., in September. The agency says it has since rerouted 180 flights. On average, each flight saved about 10 minutes and 80 gallons of fuel. On November 10, the FAA announced that SMART will now be used in the airspace of all 22 of its air traffic control centers.

The FAA stresses that humans stay in charge. A team of experts checks SMART's findings before they reach air traffic controllers, and controllers make the final decision. The air traffic controllers' union says it was not involved in designing or testing the tool. Some House Democrats have asked a government watchdog, the GAO, to review the plan. They say that safety staff had "little to no input" in building it. Airlines have raised concerns too. They worry about a sudden change and about possible extra cancellations.

1. **rolled out / roll out** (phrasal verb) — to make a new product or system available to many people. *The company plans to roll out the new app to all customers next month.*
2. **airspace** (noun) — the part of the sky that belongs to a country or is controlled by an air traffic center. *Heavy storms closed part of the airspace over the coast.*
3. **reroute** (verb) — to send something along a different path. *Police rerouted traffic because of the parade.*
4. **validate** (verb) — to check that something is correct or works well. *Engineers validate every design before it goes to production.*
5. **watchdog** (noun) — an organization that checks that others follow the rules. *A consumer watchdog warned people about the false advertising.*
6. **input** (noun) — ideas or information that people give to help make something. *The designer asked users for their input before changing the website.*
7. **concern** (noun) — a feeling of worry about something. *Parents expressed concern about the new school schedule.*

## 3. A DNA Test Connects Two Families After Eighty Years

Rodney Anderson, 81, lives in Surrey, England. For most of his life, he did not know who his father was. Now, thanks to a home DNA test, he has found out. His father was a Canadian soldier who passed through England during World War II.

The soldier was Charles Blakney, a mechanic with the New Brunswick Rangers. Charles sailed off to war in 1942, leaving a wife and a daughter at home. He came back to Canada in 1945. During the war, he had a relationship with an Englishwoman, Yvonne Anderson. Rodney was born to her in Farnham, England.

Years later, Charles's granddaughter, Bethany Allen, found out from divorce papers that her grandfather had another child in the UK. She searched for a long time. Then the DNA company Ancestry showed her a match. A man named Rodney was listed as a close relative. "Oh my god, his name is Rodney!" she said. She contacted his daughter, Dawn Roberts, through the website.

Rodney first thought it was a joke. "You can't believe what a shock it is to get that knowledge," he said. The family found other links. Rodney became an auto mechanic, just like Charles. Barb Lowe, his half-sister, says he has the "same ears" as their father. Rodney has bought plane tickets to visit New Brunswick next summer.
