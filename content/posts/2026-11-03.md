## 1. All Aboard the Maple Steam Train

Autumn colours are coming to the mountains of Alishan, and a very old train is ready to take visitors there. The Alishan Forest Railway has announced five special tours for December. Passengers will ride a historic steam locomotive, pulling carriages made of Taiwan cypress.

The tours will run on five Tuesdays: December 1, 8, 15, 22 and 29. Each one leaves Alishan Station, stops at Zhushan and Erwanping stations, and then returns. The price is NT$3,250 (about US$100) per person. That covers train tickets, meals, a guided tour, entry to the recreation area and a souvenir. Only 100 people can join each tour.

Every tour will be led by Huang Yuan-ming, a nature photographer who lives in Alishan. Passengers will be able to get off the train at Duigaoyue and Zhushan to photograph steam locomotive No. 31. At Erwanping, they can watch the locomotive turn around on a wye track, a Y-shaped section of rails that lets a train change direction.

Booking will be strict. Tickets are not sold at station counters. Everything must be booked online. Reservations for the first tour open at 10 a.m. on November 17, and each later tour opens 15 days before its departure date. Given how popular steam trains are, seats may be taken quickly.

The railway announced the plan in a press release on Monday.

## 2. A Poem from Space

Can a computer write a poem in space? Google has just shown that one can. The company launched a small test satellite on October 1 as part of an experiment called Project Suncatcher. The satellite shared a ride into space with another company's satellites on a SpaceX rocket.

Inside the satellite is a special computer chip called a TPU. A TPU is a chip designed to run artificial intelligence (AI) software. It is normally found in huge data centres on the ground. Google wants to find out whether such chips can work in orbit, where there is plenty of sunlight for power.

The test was a success. The chip ran Gemma 4, one of Google's open AI models, and asked it to write a haiku, a short Japanese-style poem, about being in space. The poem was then sent back to Earth through a receiving station in Svalbard, in Norway.

There are limits, though. The satellite carries four of these chips, but its solar panels produce only about one kilowatt of power. That is enough to run just one chip at a time, for about 20 minutes. The next step is to test how well the equipment copes with extreme heat, cold and radiation in space.

The experiment is small, but it points to a big idea: one day, AI might be processed in space instead of on the ground.

**Vocabulary**

- **orbit** (n./v.) – the path an object takes around a planet. *The satellite stays in orbit about 500 km above Earth.*
- **launch** (v.) – to send a rocket or product into the air or onto the market. *The agency will launch a new probe next year.*
- **experiment** (n.) – a test done to learn something. *The students did an experiment with magnets.*
- **data centre** (n.) – a building full of computers that store and process information. *Large data centres use a lot of electricity.*
- **limit** (n.) – the highest amount or level that is possible. *There is a limit to how much the battery can hold.*
- **cope with** (phr. v.) – to deal successfully with a difficult situation. *The bridge can cope with strong winds.*
- **radiation** (n.) – energy that travels in waves or particles, such as from the sun. *Astronauts need protection from radiation.*

**Questions**

## 3. Twenty-Eight Years to Walk Home

On November 1, 1998, a 29-year-old British former paratrooper named Karl Bushby started walking from Punta Arenas, a city at the southern end of Chile. His goal was simple: reach his mother's house in Hull, England. He set himself two rules. He had to travel under his own power, and he could not return home until he walked through her front door.

He expected the trip to take about 12 years. It took 28. Along the way he covered roughly 31,000 miles (50,000 km) across four continents.

The journey was never easy. In 2006, he and a companion, Dimitri Kieffer, crossed the Bering Strait from Alaska into Russia over shifting sea ice. Russian authorities arrested them. Later, Bushby spent almost ten years crossing Russia. In 2024 he swam across the Caspian Sea, and he also swam the English Channel to reach Britain.

This week, he finally made it. His mother, Angela, asked him one simple question: "Where have you been?"

Bushby's trip shows how a goal can turn into a way of life. A plan that began as a bold challenge for a young man became a story that lasted nearly three decades.

## 4. The Tapirs Are Back

In the Atlantic Forest of Rio de Janeiro state in Brazil, an animal that disappeared more than 100 years ago has returned. The lowland tapir is Brazil's largest native land mammal. It looks a little like a pig with a short trunk. Nobody had recorded one in the state since 1914.

The comeback is thanks to the Guapiaçu Ecological Reserve. Its workers have planted more than one million native tree seedlings, and the reserve protects around 10,000 hectares. They have restored more than 580 hectares of damaged land. Once the forest began to recover, the tapirs could return.

In 2017, the project released a female tapir named Eva as part of a conservation programme. Today, about 25 tapirs live in and around the reserve. Several young tapirs have been born in the wild, which is a sign that the animals are settling in.

Tapirs are sometimes called "gardeners of the forest." They eat fruit and then spread the seeds in their droppings, including large seeds that smaller animals cannot move. This helps new trees grow, and new trees help the whole forest.

The story shows that damaged nature can recover when people give it time, space and care.

**Vocabulary**

- **native** (adj.) – naturally living or growing in a place. *The kiwi is native to New Zealand.*
- **seedling** (n.) – a very young plant grown from a seed. *We planted tomato seedlings in spring.*
- **restore** (v.) – to bring something back to its earlier good condition. *Workers restored the old temple.*
- **degraded** (adj.) – damaged and in worse condition than before. *Degraded land cannot support many plants.*
- **conservation** (n.) – the protection of nature and wildlife. *Conservation groups work to save rare birds.*
- **settle in** (phr. v.) – to become comfortable in a new place. *The cat settled in quickly after the move.*
- **recover** (v.) – to return to a normal or healthy state. *The fish population is slowly recovering.*
