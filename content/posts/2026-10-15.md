## 1. Taiwan Asks Voters to Ban a Cruel Trap

Animal welfare groups in Taiwan have taken a big step toward a public vote. On Tuesday, they gave the Central Election Commission (CEC) more than 12,000 forms. The forms ask for a referendum on a full ban on wild boar snares.

A snare is a trap with a spring and a metal cable. It is meant to catch wild boars, which damage farmers' crops. But the groups say the traps cannot tell one animal from another. Dogs, cats, and protected wildlife can also be caught. The injuries can be terrible, and some animals lose a leg or die.

The groups collected the forms over 35 days. They sent two proposals, with about 6,000 forms for each one. The first would create a new law that bans the use, making, and sale of the traps. The second would change the Animal Protection Act.

This is not the first attempt. The CEC rejected a similar proposal in 2024, and a later court case failed. The commission said then that existing laws already covered the traps. It also said the old proposal was unclear about traditional Indigenous hunting.

The new proposals aim to avoid that problem. Traditional traps made from natural materials would not be covered. The groups also suggest other tools for farmers, such as electric fences and netting.

The CEC now has 60 days to finish its first review. If the proposals pass, the groups will need 293,228 valid signatures for each one.

## 2. A Startup Wants to Shoot Cargo into Space with a Giant Gun

Rockets are expensive, so one California startup has a surprising idea: use a gun. Longshot Space Technology, based in Alameda, wants to fire cargo into space from a very long steel pipe. The company has raised about $20 million.

The design is simple to describe. The pipe lies flat on the ground. Along its length, tanks of high-pressure gas push the object forward at exact moments. Each push makes it go faster. The idea is based on old "multi-injection" guns from World War II and the 1960s.

The object, called a projectile, is shaped like a small wing. After it leaves the pipe, the air helps it climb. So the gun does not need to point upward.

Longshot's test gun is 6 inches wide and 70 feet long, near Barstow, California. It has reached Mach 4, which means four times the speed of sound. A bigger gun, 30 inches wide, is coming soon. A later version could launch 220-pound loads at Mach 8.

The first customer is the U.S. military, which wants cheap targets to test fast missile defenses. The long-term goal is much bigger. To reach orbit, a payload would need to travel about 5 kilometers per second. That would take a gun about six miles long. The company says it could cut the cost to about $50 per kilogram.

The hardest problem, one founder says, is not physics. It is finding a place where officials will let them build it.

## 3. An AI Device That Tells Moose to Get Off the Tracks

Animals on roads, railways, and runways cause real problems. In the United States, wildlife strikes cost airlines about $272 million a year on average. In France, wandering animals affect more than 8,000 trains each year.

A Swedish startup called Flox Intelligence has a clever answer. Its small, battery-powered device is fixed to a pole or tree. It has a motion sensor. When something moves nearby, the sensor wakes a camera. Software on the device then identifies the animal. Finally, the device plays a sound that is right for that species, to scare the animal away.

There is one more trick. Animals can get used to a sound that is repeated again and again. So the system learns from experience and changes its sounds. This stops animals from ignoring the warning.

Flox began at the KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm in 2020. About 150 devices are now working in North America, Europe, and Australia. They are found at airfields, rail lines, farms, mines, and national parks. The founder, Sara Nozkova, says the company's strength is its field data. It knows what actually makes a moose or a grizzly bear move away safely.

The company expects about $1.5 million in sales this year. It is now looking for $10 million to grow from a few hundred devices to several thousand. It is also a finalist at TechCrunch Disrupt 2026.
