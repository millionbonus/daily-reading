## 1. Tsai Looks to Silicon Valley

Former President Tsai Ing-wen has called for stronger two-way ties between Taiwan and Silicon Valley. She spoke on Friday, October 10, at the Taipei Economic and Cultural Office in San Francisco. The audience was a group of young tech entrepreneurs.

Tsai had just finished a visit to Stanford University. It was her first trip to the United States since she left office. Besides accepting Stanford's invitation, she wanted to meet Taiwanese people who work, study, or start companies in the Bay Area. On the second day in a row, she also met people from the local startup and investment community to hear their views.

Tsai said Taiwan is becoming more connected to the world through semiconductors, artificial intelligence, communications technology, and biomedicine. As these links grow, she said, Taiwan should ask what else it can offer besides its products and technology.

She encouraged young Taiwanese in tech to share Taiwan's experience and stories with the world. She also asked them to bring back ideas about technology, talent, investment, and new ways of working. In her view, a country's strength depends not only on its government and businesses. It also depends on people, especially young people who are willing to take risks.

Tsai was Taiwan's first female president and served from 2016 to 2024. She arrived in the United States on Monday and is due to return to Taiwan on Sunday.

## 2. A Warning Triangle Problem for Robot Trucks

Self-driving trucks have just cleared a big legal hurdle in the United States. Two companies, Aurora Innovation and Kodiak AI, have received a federal exemption from a long-standing safety rule.

The rule says that when a truck breaks down, the driver must pull over and turn on the hazard lights. Within 10 minutes, the driver must also place reflective warning triangles on the road. This helps other drivers see the stopped truck. But a self-driving truck has no driver to do this job.

Aurora took the issue to court and sued federal safety regulators. A court denied its request for an exemption, so the company appealed to a higher court in Washington, D.C. While the appeal continued, companies had a temporary waiver, which means they were allowed to ignore the rule for a while.

Now the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration has granted a five-year exemption. Instead of triangles on the road, trucks can use warning beacons, which are bright flashing lights fixed to the cab (the part of the truck where the driver sits).

Industry leaders welcomed the news. A Kodiak executive said it will help bring an "autonomous era of freight movement." An Aurora official called the beacons a "critical, 21st-century solution" that warns other drivers without putting a person at risk. Some people now wonder whether cab-mounted beacons could one day become standard on all trucks.

## 3. Five Cheetah Cubs, Live on Camera

Five cheetah cubs were born on October 5 at the Smithsonian's Conservation Biology Institute in Front Royal, Virginia. Anyone in the world can now watch them grow on a live video stream.

The mother, Zura, is five years old and this is her first litter. The father, Flash, is nine and has already had seven cubs in earlier litters. Cheetah fathers do not help raise their young. Zura was born at the institute herself in 2021. Her mother and grandmother also live there, so the center now cares for four generations of one family.

The cubs are healthy and active. They began climbing on their mother on the first day. Like all cheetah cubs, they were born with their eyes and ears closed. The eyes usually open in four to seven days. The cubs gain about 50 grams a day. After three or four weeks, they should start following Zura outside the den.

For now, the staff are keeping their distance. Later they will weigh the cubs, check their health, and mark each one with a small shaved patch so they can tell them apart. The first vet exam and vaccinations will come at six weeks.

The birth matters for conservation. Cheetahs are listed as vulnerable, and their global numbers have fallen by more than 90 percent since 1900. The institute works with other zoos to choose breeding pairs carefully, based on DNA, health, and temperament. Flash is considered genetically valuable, so these cubs may help the species in the future.
