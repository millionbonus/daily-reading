## 1. A Long-Awaited Name: Taiwan's Siraya People

In July, Taiwan made history. The government officially recognized the Siraya as the country's 17th Indigenous group. Until then, only 16 groups had official status, and all of them were generally classed as Mountain Indigenous peoples.

The Siraya are a Plains Indigenous people. For many years, they went unrecognized. After the Kuomintang government took power in 1945, Plains Indigenous peoples were seen as people who had largely blended into the majority population. The movement to change this began in the 1990s.

A new law made the difference. The Plains Indigenous Peoples Status Act was passed by the Legislature on October 17, 2025. It gives Plains Indigenous groups the right to official recognition and to keep their collective identity.

Now people must register. Applications opened on August 13. On Wednesday, the Council of Indigenous Peoples told lawmakers that 12,178 people had applied to join the Siraya register. By the end of September, 1,336 of them, about 11 percent, had completed registration.

Applicants must prove their roots with documents. One key source is census records from the Japanese colonial period, which listed Plains communities and where they lived.

Eight other groups have also asked for recognition, including the Taivoan, Makatao, and Pazeh. Experts will review some of these applications soon.

## 2. Can Elon Musk Build a Chip Factory from Scratch?

Chips power everything from phones to AI systems. Now Elon Musk is reported to be planning his own chip factory. Media reports call it "Terafab." It would be a huge complex in Grimes County, Texas, that makes chips and also runs AI computing. The first step would reportedly be a pilot fab, a small test factory, costing about US$3 billion.

Does this threaten TSMC, the world's largest contract chipmaker? An investor and former analyst, Kirk Yang, says no. Speaking in Taipei on Wednesday, he said Terafab would have "absolutely no impact" on TSMC in the short or medium term.

His reason is simple. Building a top-level chip factory is extremely hard. Even TSMC needs several years to build a new fab and bring it up to full production. If Musk succeeds, Yang thinks the factory would take five to ten years to start operating.

Many big companies have tried and failed. Yang pointed to IBM's chip business, where he once worked. It was eventually sold to GlobalFoundries. The technology is difficult, and the money needed is enormous.

Still, Yang trusts Musk's long-term ability to deliver. He mentioned Musk's record at Tesla and SpaceX. He also expects American partners such as Intel to join, because Washington wants U.S. companies to lead chip making. In the long run, he said, Intel could even become a rival to TSMC.

### Vocabulary

- **contract chipmaker** (n.) — a company that makes chips for other firms to design. *Many phone brands rely on a contract chipmaker.*
- **fab** (n.) — short for "fabrication plant," a factory that makes chips. *The new fab will employ thousands of workers.*
- **pilot** (adj.) — small and used as a test before a larger project. *The city began a pilot program for free buses.*
- **ramp up** (phrasal v.) — to increase production step by step. *The company ramped up output before the holidays.*
- **barrier** (n.) — something that makes it hard to enter or succeed. *High costs are a barrier for new airlines.*
- **track record** (n.) — a person's or company's past results. *She has a strong track record in sales.*
- **rival** (n.) — a person or company that competes with you. *The two stores have been rivals for years.*

## 3. A Fossil Forgotten in a Drawer for 100 Years

Sometimes the biggest discoveries are hiding in plain sight. Scientists in London have named a new dinosaur from a fossil that sat unnoticed in a museum for almost a century.

The fossil is a piece of skull. An amateur fossil hunter, Reginald Walter Tooley, found it on the Isle of Wight in the UK. The Natural History Museum bought it in 1924. Then it stayed in storage, and nobody understood what it was.

A team led by University College London has now studied it again. They named the animal *Invictibellatrix amberina*, which means "unconquered female warrior." It lived about 120 million years ago. It was a megaraptoran, a type of meat-eating dinosaur with large, sharp claws.

This one was not huge. It was about three to four meters long, roughly the size of a large tiger. Its claws were probably 10 to 20 centimeters long. Scientists think it hunted small dinosaurs, lizards, and mammals, much like a hyena does today.

The find is important because it is the first megaraptoran ever found in Europe. It supports the idea that these predators spread from Asia, through Europe, and on to the southern continents. Later relatives grew much bigger. One in Argentina may have reached nine or ten meters.

Lead author Cassius Morrison said it was not the top predator of its time. But for humans, he added, it would have been more dangerous than a T. rex. The study was published in the journal *Royal Society Open Science*.
