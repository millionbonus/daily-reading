## 1. Workers at Micron in Taiwan Vote to Strike

Workers at a Micron factory in Taoyuan, Taiwan, have voted to allow a strike. The union announced the result on October 7. Of the 2,258 members who were eligible to vote, 2,012 took part. A total of 1,994 voted in favour, and only 14 voted against. That means about 99 percent of the votes cast supported a strike.

The dispute is about how the company shares its profits. Micron is making a lot of money from the boom in computer memory chips for artificial intelligence. The union wants a one-time bonus worth 83 months of salary. It also wants a permanent system in which 15 percent of operating profit is paid to workers every quarter. Micron has said that its rewards for Taiwan staff this year are equal to between 35 and 68 months of pay.

Talks led by a mediator failed in September. No strike date has been set yet, and the union says the timing is still being discussed. It has also said a strike could be called suddenly by text message.

### Vocabulary
- **strike** (noun/verb) — to stop working as a group to demand better pay or conditions. *The nurses went on strike for two days.*
- **eligible** (adj.) — allowed to do something because you meet the rules. *Only people over 18 are eligible to vote.*
- **in favour of** (phrase) — supporting something. *Most students were in favour of a shorter school day.*
- **dispute** (noun) — a serious disagreement. *The two companies are in a dispute over prices.*
- **operating profit** (noun phrase) — money a company earns from its main business, before some costs such as tax. *The shop's operating profit grew by ten percent.*
- **mediator** (noun) — a neutral person who helps two sides reach an agreement. *A mediator helped the neighbours stop arguing.*
- **permanent** (adj.) — lasting for a long time or forever. *She finally got a permanent job.*

### Questions
1. How many members voted against the strike?
2. What are the two main things the union is asking for?
3. Why might the union want to be able to call a strike "suddenly"? (Inference)

## 2. Nobel Prize in Medicine: Using Light to Control the Brain

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine has been awarded to three scientists: Karl Deisseroth of Stanford University, and Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel from Germany. They were honoured for their work on light-gated ion channels and a technique called optogenetics.

The story began with a tiny single-celled alga. Hegemann and Nagel discovered a protein in it, called channelrhodopsin, that reacts to light. Later, Deisseroth turned this protein into a kind of light-controlled switch for nerve cells.

With optogenetics, researchers can switch the activity of individual nerve cells on or off inside a living brain. Because the method is so precise, it has become a powerful tool for studying how the brain works. The announcement was made on Monday, October 5, in Stockholm.

This prize is a good example of how basic research on a simple organism can lead to a major scientific breakthrough years later.

### Vocabulary
- **be awarded** (phrasal verb, passive) — to be given a prize or honour. *The film was awarded the top prize.*
- **honour** (verb) — to show great respect by giving a prize. *The city honoured the firefighters with a ceremony.*
- **protein** (noun) — a natural substance in living things that does many jobs. *Eggs are a good source of protein.*
- **react to** (phrasal verb) — to change or respond when something happens. *Plants react to light by growing towards it.*
- **precise** (adj.) — very exact and accurate. *The doctor gave us precise instructions.*
- **breakthrough** (noun) — an important discovery or development. *Scientists announced a breakthrough in cancer research.*
- **organism** (noun) — a living thing. *A bacterium is a very small organism.*

### Questions
1. Where did Hegemann and Nagel find the protein channelrhodopsin?
2. What did Deisseroth do with the protein?
3. Why does the article say basic research on a simple organism can be valuable? (Inference)

## 3. Nobel Prize in Physics: Catching "Ghost" Particles Under the Ice

One day later, the Nobel Prize in Physics was announced. It went to Francis Halzen, a Belgian-born professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. He was recognised for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and for the discovery of high-energy neutrinos that come from far outside our planet.

Neutrinos are tiny particles that are very hard to detect. They pass through almost everything, including our bodies, without leaving a trace, so they are sometimes called "ghost" particles. Halzen had an unusual idea: use the clear glacial ice at the South Pole to capture them.

The result was IceCube, a huge detector buried in Antarctic ice. When a neutrino hits the ice, it can produce a faint flash of light, which the detector records. Thanks to this system, scientists can now trace neutrinos back to distant parts of the universe. The observatory has opened a new window on space.

### Vocabulary
- **decisive** (adj.) — having a very important effect on the result. *Her goal was decisive for the team.*
- **observatory** (noun) — a place with equipment for studying space or nature. *We visited an observatory on the mountain.*
- **detect** (verb) — to discover or notice something that is hard to see. *The alarm detected smoke.*
- **trace** (noun) — a small sign that something was there. *The thief left no trace.*
- **faint** (adj.) — weak and not easy to see or hear. *We saw a faint light in the distance.*
- **trace back to** (phrasal verb) — to find the origin of something. *Police traced the call back to a phone in Osaka.*
- **glacial** (adj.) — made of or related to glaciers (very old, large masses of ice). *The lake has clear glacial water.*

### Questions
1. Why are neutrinos called "ghost" particles?
2. Where is the IceCube detector, and what is it made of?
3. What does the phrase "a new window on space" suggest about IceCube's importance? (Inference)

## 4. A Caiman in a Wig: The Funniest Wildlife Photos of 2026

If you need a smile, look at the finalists of this year's Nikon Comedy Wildlife Awards. The competition received more than 10,000 entries from 112 countries, the highest number in its history.

This year's shortlist includes 40 individual photographs, 4 portfolio entries, 9 videos, and 3 entries from photographers under 16. One of the most talked-about images shows a caiman, a reptile related to the crocodile, that seems to be wearing an Elvis-style wig. Other pictures catch animals in strange poses and surprising moments that look like jokes.

The awards have a serious side, too. The organisers hope that humorous pictures will make people care more about nature and protect it. The finalists were revealed in early October, and the winners will be announced on December 8. Until then, fans can enjoy the shortlist and guess who will take the prize.
