## 1. Penguins Take a Walk in Taipei

On Thursday morning, Taipei Zoo held its first-ever "penguin parade." African penguins left their home and waddled along a special path while a large crowd watched. The walk lasted only 15 minutes and began at 9:30 a.m.

The turnout was bigger than the zoo had expected. Some visitors reportedly arrived as early as 8 a.m. to get a good spot. By 9 a.m., the line stretched from the zoo entrance all the way to the Maokong gondola station.

The walk is not just for fun. According to the zoo, exercise lowers the penguins' risk of bumblefoot, a foot problem that can develop when animals do not move enough. Zookeepers also gave a short talk, so visitors could learn about the birds while they watched.

A zoo researcher, Wang Yi-min, described the event as a kind of trial. The zoo wanted to see how visitors reacted and how the 43 penguins that took part coped with the crowd. Because space near the enclosure is limited, changes may be made in the future. One idea is to hand out numbered tickets. Another is to hold the walks on days when the Penguin House is closed.

Visitors will also face strict rules: keep the noise down, do not chase, touch, or feed the penguins, and avoid sudden movements. The idea seems to come from a famous penguin parade at Asahiyama Zoo in Japan. African penguins are smaller than that zoo's king penguins, and Taipei's walks will only be held when the temperature is below 24°C.

## 2. Google Launches a Faster, Cheaper AI Model

Google has released a new artificial intelligence (AI) model called Gemini 4 Neon. The company says it brings "frontier-level" intelligence, which means it is close to the best AI available today. Google also says it can finish tasks quickly, such as writing software, handling office work, and helping with science projects.

Neon sits in the middle of the Gemini 4 family. A larger model, Carbon, handles the hardest long projects. A smaller one, Hydrogen, is made for fast everyday jobs. Neon aims to balance depth and cost.

Price matters here. Developers who use the model pay $3 for every million input "tokens" and $15 for every million output tokens. A token is a small piece of text, roughly a word or part of a word. Neon can also read up to one million tokens at once. That is enough to look at long documents, spreadsheets, and even audio or video in a single request.

Users can choose among four "thinking levels." A higher level makes the model reason for longer before it answers. Google says the cost per task stays much lower than that of rival models set to their top levels.

Google reports strong test scores. According to an independent ranking site, Artificial Analysis, Neon placed third overall. Note that many of these results come from Google's own announcement, so it is wise to wait for outside tests. The model is available to developers, to businesses, and to paying users of the Gemini app. A separate version for cyber security defenders will be offered to a small group of approved users.

## 3. Your Cat Is Asking You for Help

Anyone who lives with a cat knows the look. The cat stares at its bowl, then at you, then at the bowl again. A new study suggests this is not an accident. Cats seem to know that humans can solve problems for them.

Researchers in Brazil tested 38 cats. Each cat could see its favorite treat inside a clear container, but could not open it. The researchers set up the problem twice. In one test, an attentive person stood nearby. In the other, the cat was alone.

The results were clear. When a person was present, the cats meowed 86 times during the observation periods. When they were alone, they meowed only 20 times. The researchers calculated that a cat was more than 16 times as likely to meow when a human was available to help.

The cats also looked back and forth between the food and the person. Scientists call this "gaze alternation." Young children do the same thing when a toy is on a high shelf. They look at the toy, then at a parent, as if to say, "Please get that for me."

The study was published in the journal *Animal Behaviour*. It adds to growing evidence that pet cats are better at communicating with people than many owners once believed.
